A pattern we see repeatedly in property claims. Across a broad sample of property claims, the gap that most consistently predicts downstream problems isn't in the inspection or the settlement. It's in the three days between when a claim is filed and when the adjuster actually reaches the property. When that window is tight, files tend to close cleanly and stay closed. When it stretches, three things start to happen — none of them good. The insured starts calling to ask what's happening. Trust erodes before the adjuster has even arrived. Damage evolves. Water spreads. Smoke residue sets. Contents get moved. The condition captured at inspection isn't the condition of the original loss. Documentation from other sources arrives first. Contractors, public adjusters, or the insured's own photos start shaping the narrative before the adjuster has assessed it independently. None of this is dramatic on any single file. Compounded across a book of claims, it's the difference between a program that runs steadily and one that spends its year chasing supplements. The partners who win on cycle time in the middle of the process usually built the win into the first three days. #ClaimsWithAspen #ClaimsIntelligence #ClaimsOperations

When Aspen starts working with a new carrier, my first question is almost always the same. What's the file you regret the most from last year — and why? It sounds like a strange thing to lead with. It's not. What a claims leader remembers as their worst file usually reveals more about how their operation actually works than any performance metric they could show me. Some carriers name a specific catastrophic loss. Others name a routine file that spiraled. Some name a complaint they still feel personally about. What they all share is the moment the leader knew something had gone wrong — and where the system failed them. That's the diagnostic. Because whatever produced last year's regretted file is almost always still in the operation, waiting to produce the next one. It might be a workflow bottleneck. It might be a vendor handoff. It might be a communication gap that only shows up under specific conditions. The best claims operations I've seen aren't the ones without regretted files. They're the ones where the leader can name what changed after the regretted file happened. If you're heading into next year's planning cycle, that question is worth asking yourself before anyone asks it of you. #ClaimsWithAspen #AspenVoices #ClaimsLeadership

You can spot a well-run field operation in the first fifteen minutes of a deployment. Here's what you're looking at. I've spent enough time in the field across enough events to know within the first fifteen minutes of arriving whether a deployment is going to run well or run rough. The tells are small. Most of them aren't things you'd notice unless you'd been on both sides of them — a well-run event and one that unraveled — and even then they're easy to miss if you're focused on the wrong things. This is a piece for claims leaders who don't spend time in the field but need to know what good looks like when they're evaluating a vendor's field capabilities. It's also for the field adjusters who are trying to work out what separates a deployment they'll want to come back to from one they won't. The first tell is how people are briefed Every deployment starts with a briefing. The briefing tells you almost everything you need to know about how the operation runs. A weak briefing sounds like this: coverage territory, claim volume, filing deadlines, contact numbers, hotel information. Twenty minutes, everyone leaves with the same generic information, and questions get worked out later on individual calls. A strong briefing sounds different. The person leading it knows which adjusters are on the deployment by name. They've thought about which routes go to whom based on distance and complexity. They flag the specific carrier requirements that differ from what the team has been working on. They give the field team permission to escalate — with a specific person to escalate to and a phone number that will actually get answered. The difference isn't in what gets covered. It's in whether the person leading it has actually thought about the specific event they're briefing on, or whether they're running a template. The second tell is how the schedule works Bad field operations run inspection schedules on rigid time blocks that don't reflect what any given inspection actually requires. Every property gets the same slot. When one runs long — and one always runs long — the entire day cascades. Good field operations schedule against complexity, not against clock. Simple losses in tight windows. Complex losses with buffers. Multi-property routes clustered geographically. The dispatcher knows which properties are two-story, which need ladder assist, and which have policyholder access constraints that will slow down the front end. When you're an adjuster running an eight-inspection day, the difference between the two approaches is roughly whether you finish the day at 5:30 with clean files, or at 8:00 with three files half-documented and a plan to catch up tomorrow. The third tell is how escalations get handled Every field deployment produces situations that need a decision from someone above the adjuster level. Coverage questions. Access disputes. Complex causation calls. Policyholder situations that require special handling. The measure of a field operation is how fast those escalations move. In a well-run operation, the adjuster calls a specific person who has authority to make the call. The answer comes back within hours. The adjuster keeps moving. In a weak operation, the escalation gets routed through a queue. It sits. The adjuster either waits — which stalls the file — or makes their best guess and moves on, which creates a downstream problem the file will have to solve later. Speed of escalation is one of the most measurable indicators of field operations health, and it's rarely tracked as a metric. It should be. The fourth tell is what happens between inspection and submission This is the part most people outside field operations don't think about. Between the adjuster leaving the property and the file reaching the carrier desk, a lot happens — and how it happens tells you what the vendor is optimizing for. A weak operation lets adjusters draft files at whatever cadence they naturally work at. Some submit within twenty-four hours. Some take three days. Consistency isn't managed. A strong operation has an inspection-to-submission target that everyone works to — usually within twenty-four to forty-eight hours — and structures the review process to make that target achievable. Support staff help with scope entry. QA reviewers are available quickly. The system is built around the reality that a fresh file writes better than a stale one. The fifth tell is how adjusters talk about the operation This one is subjective but consistent. Adjusters who work in strong field operations describe the work in a specific way. They talk about the file, the property, the loss, the policyholder. They don't spend much time talking about internal friction. Adjusters in weaker operations talk about the operation itself. The scheduling. The QA turnaround. The communication gaps. The workarounds they've developed. When you ask a vendor to let you talk to their field adjusters, this is the signal to listen for. It tells you what the day-to-day experience of working on the deployment actually is — which tells you a lot about what the files coming out of that deployment will look like. What this means for claims leaders Most vendor evaluations focus on capabilities, technology, and coverage. Those matter. But they're the part of the operation that shows up in the RFP. The five tells above are what shows up in the deployment itself, and they're the ones that decide how the files actually land on your desk. Worth asking about before the next event. — Brent Morrow Operations Manager, Aspen Claims Service

Rather than a single deep-dive on one regulatory shift, worth pulling back for a broader view of what's moved this quarter. Three quiet developments from Q2 that changed the operational file standard across multiple states, without generating much industry conversation. Louisiana — post-Ida claims handling guidance. The state issued clarifying language on continuing-adjustment obligations for files where reconstruction has extended past normal windows. Documentation requirements for extended-adjustment files are now closer to what regulators expect on active files, not archived ones. North Carolina — updated appraisal panel disclosure requirements. New expectations around who qualifies as an appraiser, what disclosures the panel members must make, and how the appraisal process interacts with pending litigation. Operational shift is small; the paperwork trail is meaningful. California — updated ALE guidance on displacement extensions. The state clarified how additional living expense should be calculated when displacement extends beyond the initial coverage window. Vendors and carriers with California exposure should be tracking this — the interpretation matters. None of these developments made industry headlines. All of them changed something about how a compliant file needs to be built. If your operation's regulatory tracking still runs on major-headline monitoring, this is the quarter to add a broader sweep to the workflow. #ClaimsWithAspen #ClaimsIntelligence #InsuranceRegulation

Aspen Claims ServiceProperty Claims ServicesScheduled · 26 Aug 🌐

Four words that appear in most claims workflows and quietly do more damage than most operations realize. "We'll get back to you." Said by the adjuster to the policyholder. Said by the carrier desk to the adjuster. Said by the vendor to the carrier. Every version of the phrase adds a beat to the file's timeline and takes something off the account of trust the customer is holding. The problem isn't the delay itself. The problem is the ambiguity. "We'll get back to you" leaves the receiver holding the uncertainty — not knowing when, not knowing whether to follow up, not knowing whether the file is actually moving. The operations that handle this well replaced the phrase years ago. Instead of "we'll get back to you," they say "we'll have an answer by Thursday at 3pm." Small difference. Enormous downstream effect. Specificity closes cognitive loops. Ambiguity leaves them open. Multiply that across a thousand-file book and the operational cost adds up. The claims operations winning on satisfaction scores right now aren't necessarily faster than the ones losing on them. They're just more specific. #ClaimsWithAspen #ClaimsOperations #PolicyholderExperience

Mon · 31 AugStatic · The complaint that never gets filed
Aspen Claims ServiceProperty Claims ServicesScheduled · 31 Aug 🌐
+ Follow
The most useful data point in your claims operation is one most carriers never track. For every formal complaint that gets filed against a claim, there are usually multiple policyholders who were unhappy enough to consider complaining and didn't. They chose not to escalate. They renewed anyway, or they didn't. Either way, their dissatisfaction never entered the system as data. This invisible complaint volume is where most of the real customer experience story lives. Carriers with formal complaint rates that look fine can still have retention problems, referral gaps, and reputational drag that trace back to files where policyholders felt something went wrong but didn't push it further. Three ways to surface the invisible signal: Post-close surveys with open-response fields. Not just NPS scores. Ask what the policyholder wishes had been different. Read the free text. Adjuster debriefs on difficult files. Adjusters know which files had unhappy policyholders long before any complaint would get filed. Ask them. Renewal data cross-referenced with claim history. Policyholders who filed a claim and didn't renew are telling you something. The reasons don't always get captured, but the correlation does. The claims operations pulling ahead on retention are the ones treating the complaint that didn't get filed as data worth chasing. It's the earliest signal available. It's also the one most operations aren't looking at. #ClaimsWithAspen #ClaimsIntelligence #PolicyholderExperience

The word "partner" has been overused in vendor conversations to the point of losing meaning. It's finally starting to mean something again — and here's what actually distinguishes a partner from a vendor. For most of my career, the word "partner" in vendor conversations meant nothing. It was the polite thing carriers called vendors when they wanted the relationship to feel less transactional than it was. Everyone knew it. Nobody said it. The word did some marketing work and moved on. Something has shifted over the last two years. The word is starting to mean something again — and the vendors and carriers who understand what it means now are pulling ahead of the ones still using it as a decorative term. Here's what I've come to believe distinguishes a real partnership from a vendor relationship dressed up in partnership language. A partner is measured on outcomes the vendor doesn't fully control A vendor is measured on their deliverables. Cycle time. Cost per file. SLA compliance. These are things the vendor can control end-to-end — inputs go in, outputs come out, performance is measurable. A partner is measured on outcomes that require both parties to get right. Reopen rate. Policyholder satisfaction. Retention on claim-affected policies. Total lifecycle claim cost. These aren't things the vendor can control alone. They require the carrier's workflow to work with the vendor's workflow, and they measure whether the combined system is producing the outcomes the carrier actually cares about. When a carrier starts measuring a vendor on outcomes the vendor can't fully control, they're not being unfair. They're signaling that the relationship has moved from transactional to structural. A partner is invited into problems, not just tasks A vendor gets assignments. Files come in, work gets done, files go out. The relationship stays inside a defined scope of work. A partner gets invited into problems the carrier is trying to solve. How do we improve our CAT response? How do we bring down our supplement volume? How do we design a better file standard? These conversations happen outside the SOW. They happen because the carrier trusts the vendor to think about their business, not just to execute against it. The invitation is the tell. When a vendor gets asked to weigh in on strategic questions, the relationship has moved somewhere different than a normal contract. A partner tells the carrier things they don't want to hear This is the one that separates the real partnerships from the ones performing partnership language. A vendor delivers what the carrier asks for. If the carrier asks for aggressive cycle times, the vendor optimizes for aggressive cycle times. If the carrier asks for a specific file standard, the vendor delivers to that standard. The relationship stays professional, deliverable-focused, and pleasant. A partner sometimes tells the carrier that what they're asking for is going to cost them elsewhere. That the cycle time they want will produce reopens they don't want. That the file standard they specified is missing a documentation element that will show up as a supplement six months later. That the vendor they're evaluating for CAT capacity has structural problems they should know about. These are uncomfortable conversations. Vendors avoid them because they're not their place. Partners have them because they know that if they don't, the carrier will pay a downstream cost that the vendor could have flagged. The willingness to be uncomfortable is the highest-signal indicator I know of that a partnership has actually formed. A partner is stable through leadership changes Vendor relationships live inside org charts. When a claims VP changes, or a procurement director moves on, the vendor relationship often gets reevaluated. The relationship was with the person, not with the institution. Partnerships survive personnel changes. They survive because they've been documented in structures other than a single relationship — joint operating rhythms, shared metrics dashboards, escalation protocols that outlast any one leader. The next VP inherits a working relationship, not an evaluation cycle. This is one of the practical tests for whether a partnership is real. Ask what happens to the vendor relationship if the current claims VP leaves. If the answer is honest — "it would probably get reviewed" — that's a vendor relationship, and there's nothing wrong with that. If the answer is "the working rhythm continues regardless" — that's structural, and that's a partnership. A partner is willing to walk away This one is counterintuitive. But it's true. A vendor is incentivized to keep the relationship going regardless of fit, because their revenue depends on it. A partner is willing to say, at some point, that a particular piece of work isn't the right fit for their operation and should be handled elsewhere. That willingness to walk away is what makes the working relationship trustworthy. If a vendor will take any assignment, their advice is worth less. If a vendor will decline work that's not right for them, their willingness to take on the work you do give them means something. The best partners I've watched work are also the ones most willing to be honest about the limits of what they should be doing. Why this matters now The industry is heading into a period where the difference between a vendor relationship and a partnership is going to matter more than it has in a long time. Regulatory pressure is increasing. Reinsurance costs are shifting. Policyholder expectations are recalibrating. Carriers that navigate these shifts well are going to be the ones with real partners on the vendor side, not just capable vendors. The word "partner" is finally starting to mean something because the operational stakes have gotten high enough to require it. Worth reading the vendor conversations in your own operation with those distinctions in mind. — Mark Vetrovec CEO, Aspen Claims Service

Fri · 11 SepStatic · September Post 1 — the 90-day CAT read
Aspen Claims ServiceProperty Claims ServicesScheduled · 11 Sep 🌐
+ Follow
The most useful CAT deployment metric doesn't get its full read until 90 days after the event closes. Cycle time during the deployment tells one story. Supplement volume in the 30, 60, and 90-day windows after closure tells a different one — and the two stories often contradict each other. The pattern most common in post-event data: Files closed fastest during the deployment produce the highest supplement rates in the 60-day window. Vendors ranked highest on cycle time often rank differently when 90-day supplement volume is included in the ranking. The total cost of a CAT deployment isn't calculable until this window has passed. Most carrier CAT reporting stops when the deployment closes. The post-event report gets filed, the team moves on, and the supplement volume that shows up in the following weeks gets absorbed into general quarterly numbers — disconnected from the deployment that produced it. The carriers who understand their real CAT performance are the ones who keep tracking specific event-linked supplement volume through the 90-day window. It's a small change in reporting discipline. It produces a materially more honest picture of which vendors and which operational choices actually worked. Worth adding to this year's post-mortem before the data starts to blur. #ClaimsWithAspen #ClaimsIntelligence #CATResponse
